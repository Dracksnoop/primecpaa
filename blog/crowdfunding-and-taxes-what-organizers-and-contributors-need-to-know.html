Crowdfunding and Taxes: What Organizers and Contributors Need to Know

Crowdfunding has opened the door for people to raise money for almost anything: medical bills, family emergencies, creative projects, business startups, and even investment opportunities. But the tax rules depend heavily on why the money was raised, what the contributor received in return, and how the platform processed the payment. For taxpayers, the key lesson is simple: crowdfunding is not automatically a tax-free “gift,” and it is not automatically taxable income either. The tax result depends on the facts and circumstances.

If you are involved in a crowdfunding campaign as either the organizer or the contributor, the three most common categories to understand are:

  • Donation-based campaigns

  • Rewards-based campaigns

  • Equity-based campaigns

Each one creates different tax consequences.

Donation-Based Crowdfunding: Personal Causes vs. Charitable Causes

Donation-based crowdfunding is often used for personal needs such as medical bills, funeral expenses, disaster recovery, or living expenses. It is also used for charitable fundraising. These two situations may look similar online, but they are treated very differently for tax purposes.

  • Personal Donation Campaigns - If a crowdfunding campaign is set up for an individual’s personal hardship and the contributor gives money without expecting anything meaningful in return, the payment received may qualify as a gift rather than taxable income. Under the gift standard, the payment must come from “detached and disinterested generosity” and not be made in exchange for goods, services, or other economic benefit. In practice, campaigns raised to help with medical bills are often treated as nontaxable gifts when the facts fit that standard.

    o    Organizer: For the organizer, this means the funds may not be taxable if they truly qualify as gifts. But the organizer should not assume that a crowdfunding page label like “donation” automatically controls the tax result. The IRS looks at the actual facts, including whether the contributor expected something of value in return.

    o    Contributor: For the contributor, a personal gift to another individual is generally not deductible as a charitable contribution. That is an important point: even if the payment feels charitable or compassionate, it usually does not qualify for a tax deduction unless it was made to a qualified charity.

  • Charitable Donation Campaigns - A different result applies when the crowdfunding campaign is run by a qualified section 501(c)(3) charitable organization. In that situation, the donor may be able to claim a charitable contribution deduction if all normal charitable rules are satisfied including an IRS qualified donation receipt. But if the donor receives goods or services in return, part or all of the payment may be nondeductible.

    For example, if a donor gives $100 to a charity crowdfunding campaign and receives a shirt worth $25, only the amount above the fair market value of the shirt may be deductible, assuming all other requirements are met. The fair market value of any goods or services given back to the donor should be determined in advance and disclosed.

    For the organizer, the charitable status matters a great deal. Contributions to a private individual are not charitable gifts to a qualified charity, even if the campaign is framed as “helping out”. For the contributor, the deduction depends on whether the recipient is a qualified charity and whether the donor received anything of value in return.

Rewards-Based Crowdfunding: Usually Taxable to the Organizer

Rewards-based crowdfunding is the model used by platforms like Kickstarter and Indiegogo. Contributors give money in exchange for a product, service, or other reward. The reward might be a T-shirt, coffee mug, event ticket, digital content, or early access to a product.

This type of campaign is usually much more like a sale than a gift. If the contributor receives a reward with real value, the payment generally is not a gift because there is a quid pro quo.

  • Organizer Tax Treatment - For the organizer, rewards-based crowdfunding is often taxable income. The money received is generally included in gross income unless the organizer can show that some exclusion applies. If the campaign supports a business or trade, the organizer will usually report the proceeds as business income.

    The organizer may generally deduct ordinary and necessary expenses connected to the campaign if the activity is carried on with a profit motive and qualifies as a trade or business. Those expenses can include:

    o   platform fees

    o   production costs for the rewards

    o   marketing costs

    o   other ordinary business expenses.

    If the crowdfunding effort is not a for-profit activity, deductions may be limited or unavailable under the business rules. In other words, an organizer should not assume that every cost is deductible simply because the campaign was expensive to run.

  • Contributor Tax Treatment - For the contributor, the tax issue is usually not deduction-based. Instead, the question is whether the payment is a purchase, a donation, or something else. If the contributor gets merchandise or another tangible benefit, the payment is generally treated as consideration for what was received, not as a deductible charitable gift.

    If the backer receives only a token item of insubstantial value, the facts may still support gift treatment in some situations. But taxpayers should be careful here: many crowdfunding rewards are more than token items, and the presence of a real reward generally weakens any gift argument.

Equity-Based Crowdfunding: An Investment, Not a Gift

Equity crowdfunding is different from donation-based or rewards-based campaigns because the contributor receives an ownership interest, such as stock or a partnership interest, in exchange for the contribution.

  • Organizer Tax Treatment - For the organizer, money received in exchange for an equity interest is generally a capital contribution, not taxable income at receipt. The organizer is bringing in investors rather than customers or donors. That means the campaign is being treated like a real investment transaction, not a gift or sale of merchandise.

    However, this also means the organizer must treat the arrangement as an actual securities offering with related reporting and compliance obligations. The SEC crowdfunding rules require use of a registered intermediary, disclosure filings, annual reporting, and other investor protection requirements. That is not a small issue. A business that takes equity crowdfunding seriously must also take its compliance obligations seriously.

  • Contributor Tax Treatment - For the contributor, equity crowdfunding is not a charitable contribution. It is an investment. The contributor’s tax basis in the investment is generally the amount contributed. That basis matters later if the investor sells the interest or receives distributions.

    Contributors should also understand that investment returns may create future taxable income in the form of dividends, capital gains, or other taxable proceeds depending on the facts. In other words, equity crowdfunding may offer growth potential, but it also creates the same tax issues that come with any investment.

  • A Quick Note on SEC Crowdfunding Rules - If a crowdfunding campaign is offering investors an ownership interest, it may fall under SEC crowdfunding rules. Under these rules, a business can generally raise up to $5 million in a 12-month period through a registered online intermediary.

    For investors, the amount they can put in depends on income and net worth. If either their income or net worth is less than $124,000, they can invest the greater of $2,500 or 5% of the higher of their income or net worth. If both their income and net worth are $124,000 or more, they can invest up to 10% of the higher of their income or net worth, with a maximum of $124,000 in a 12-month period.

The 1099-K Issue: Just Because You Receive the Form Doesn’t Mean It’s Taxable

A common source of confusion is the Form 1099-K. Crowdfunding platforms and payment processors may issue 1099-K forms when the reporting thresholds are met. For 2025 and later reporting years, the general federal third-party settlement threshold is more than $20,000 and more than 200 transactions.

This is important because taxpayers sometimes assume a 1099-K automatically means the entire amount is taxable income. It does not. A form 1099-K is an information reporting document, not a final tax determination. If a taxpayer receives one in error or for a nontaxable campaign, the taxpayer should still report the return appropriately and keep records supporting the correct tax treatment.

For organizers, this means good recordkeeping is essential. Track who contributed, how much was received, whether anything was given back, and how the funds were used. For contributors, it means keeping records of what you gave, whether you received anything in return, and whether the recipient was a qualified charity and provided a mandated IRS donation receipt.

Gift Tax Considerations Can Apply Too

Even when a crowdfunding payment qualifies as a gift for income tax purposes, the gift tax rules may still matter. If a person makes a gift above the annual exclusion amount to one recipient, a gift tax return may be required. The annual exclusion for 2026 is $19,000 per donee.

That does not mean tax is always owed when the exclusion is exceeded. It means the donor may need to file a Form 709, which is the donor’s responsibility, not the recipient’s. This is especially important in larger family or personal support campaigns where one person contributes a substantial amount to another individual.

Practical Takeaways

Here is the simplest way to think about crowdfunding tax treatment:

  • Personal Donation Campaigns may be treated as gifts if the donor does not receive anything in return, but they are not tax-deductible charitable contributions unless the payment is made to a qualified charity.

  • Charitable Crowdfunding can be deductible, but only if the organization is a qualified section 501(c)(3) charity and the donor meets the normal substantiation rules and an IRS qualified donation receipt is provided.

  • Rewards-Based Campaigns are usually taxable to the organizer because they are more like sales than gifts.

  • Equity-Based Campaigns are generally capital contributions to the organizer and investments for the contributor.

  • Form 1099-K reporting does not decide whether the money is taxable; the underlying facts control.

Final Thoughts

Crowdfunding can be a powerful way to raise money, but tax treatment depends on substance, not labels. It may seem simple on the surface, but the tax rules can quickly become complicated depending on how the campaign is structured and what the contributor receives in return. If you are involved in a crowdfunding campaign and want to understand the tax consequences, this office can help with guidance tailored to your situation so you can avoid surprises and move forward with confidence.
